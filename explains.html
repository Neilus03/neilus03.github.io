π5 EXPLAINS · 01

Gaussian splatting,
in five minutes.

A scene is not stored as a pile of pixels. It is a cloud of soft, coloured 3D ellipses that blend into an image.

01 / 03 Many tiny blobs,
one coherent view. Move your cursor through the scene

01 · ONE GAUSSIAN

A soft 3D ellipsoid
becomes a 2D ellipse.

Each point in the cloud is a tiny, fuzzy volume. Its centre is μ; its shape and direction are its covariance Σ. From a camera, that volume lands on the screen as an ellipse.

ONE SPLAT · SCREEN SPACE μ = (0.00, 0.00)

Drag the centre. The sliders change its projected footprint.

EDIT THE GAUSSIAN

Important: a Gaussian is not a coloured disc pasted on an image. It has a 3D position, 3D extent, orientation, colour, and opacity. This ellipse is its camera-facing footprint.

02 · MANY GAUSSIANS

Move the camera.
The cloud makes a new view.

The same 3D Gaussians are projected from a new viewpoint, ordered from back to front, and alpha-blended. Nothing is hallucinated per pixel: it is fast rasterisation of learned geometry.

Geometry view: the left panel is the 3D cloud; the right panel shows the projected ellipses before they are blended.

  1. 01

    Project each 3D ellipsoid through the current camera.

  2. 02

    Sort the 2D footprints by depth, far to near.

  3. 03

    Blend their translucent colours into the final image.

03 · LEARNING THE CLOUD

Training adjusts the blobs,
not the rules of rendering.

Start with a rough cloud. Render it from a known photo position. Compare the render with that photo. Then nudge every Gaussian’s position, shape, colour, and opacity until the views agree.

∴

Why it matters: after training, a new view only needs projection, depth sorting, and alpha blending. That is why Gaussian splatting can be both geometric and real-time.
